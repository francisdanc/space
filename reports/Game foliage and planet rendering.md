# Replace distant forest triangles with impostors

The most useful change for this engine is to stop spending triangles on distant forest. Shipped large-world games all use the same layered far field: real meshes near the camera, then one cheap impostor per tree drawn from the same placement data for kilometres, then a forest material splatted into the terrain. Serious Sam 4 does exactly this across a 128 x 128 km world, and the project is missing the middle layer. It goes straight from merged mesh tier 2 to a flat canopy colour, which is the classic cause of "bald terrain from altitude" and almost certainly where most of the ~30M plant triangles go. For leaves near the camera, the industry standard is still alpha-tested branch-cluster cards with tight cut outlines. These are shaded with wrapped back-lighting (a transmission term, as in Crysis and Unreal's Two Sided Foliage), smoothed crown normals and baked crown-depth AO, and animated with hierarchical pivot wind. Unreal 5.7's experimental Nanite Foliage, which uses modelled leaves, voxel LOD and bone wind, is where things are heading, but it confirms the same lessons: alpha masking and per-vertex wind are the expensive parts, and distant foliage needs a representation that keeps its volume. On the planet side the existing architecture is sound: CPU-meshed quadtree cube-sphere, skirts, f64 positions with camera-relative f32 rendering, and Bevy's reverse-Z float depth. The cheap, high-value additions are CDLOD-style geomorphing, horizon culling, screen-space-error refinement, parent-to-child height reuse and a prioritised, cancellable job queue. Most expected-gain figures below are engineering estimates. The few measured numbers in the literature are cited where they exist, and the conclusions depend on profiling this engine's own frame.

## Leaves are still cut cards with wrapped transmission and pivot wind

The proven way to represent leaves, used from Crysis (2007) through Horizon Zero Dawn and most UE4 titles, is **alpha-tested branch-cluster cards**: atlases of leaf sprays rendered from 3D models, placed on a tree skeleton. Epic's documentation for UE 5.7 Nanite Foliage names exactly why it is moving away from cards. Alpha masking "causes heavy overdraw and an expensive mask function, and keeps foliage as flat cards". World Position Offset wind adds per-vertex cost, forces conservative culling bounds and splits materials into separate raster bins ([UE Nanite Foliage](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/nanite-foliage)). Epic's replacement has three parts. **Assemblies** instance branch parts within a tree, which cut one tree from about 3.5 GB to about 29 MB on disk. **Voxels** replace distant clusters with 4x4x4 bricks so the crown keeps its silhouette instead of decaying into "sparse, surviving triangles". **Bone-driven wind** costs about 0.1 ms per 100k bones ([UE Nanite Foliage](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/nanite-foliage)). One user test went from **62 to 119 fps** on 77,376 heavy trees with voxels enabled ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/get-a-glimpse-of-nanite-foliage-with-voxel-representation-in-ue5-7)). None of this is available in Bevy: Bevy's meshlet renderer does not support alpha-masked materials ([Bevy MeshletMesh docs](https://dev-docs.bevyengine.org/bevy/pbr/experimental/meshlet/struct.MeshletMesh.html)). The part worth borrowing is Nanite's per-voxel stochastic normal, which picks one normal per pixel from a stored distribution and lets temporal anti-aliasing (TAA) average the result. It is cheap to copy in a far crown shader so distant canopies shade with roughness rather than looking like flat impostors.

The engine's 2x2 atlas with solid inner crown volumes is already a sensible hybrid. The opaque core fills the crown cheaply without alpha. Two card refinements remain. First, cut each card outline to the opaque area with a 5-8 vertex polygon instead of a quad. A hull fitted this way removed **about 30% of pixels with 5 vertices** on impostor sprites ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/a-forest-of-octahedral-impostors/85735)), and the same geometry applies to leaf cards. Second, enlarge the atlas beyond four variants once the atlas is baked from 3D sprays, so it can carry normal and thickness channels as well as albedo.

Shading formulas from the Crysis chapter of GPU Gems 3 port directly to WGSL. The view-through-light lobe is `saturate(dot(E,-L))^4` and the wrapped back diffuse is `saturate(dot(-N,L)*0.6+0.4)`. These are lerped together and scaled by a low-resolution thickness (subsurface) map. The whole back term is multiplied by the shadow factor so shadowed leaves do not glow, and the final colour is multiplied by vertex-alpha AO ([GPU Gems 3 ch.16](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems3/part-iii-rendering/chapter-16-vegetation-procedural-animation-and-shading-crysis)). Unreal's Two Sided Foliage model works the same way. Light hitting one side also lights the back, filtered by a Subsurface Color (often just a saturated or darkened albedo), with a view-dependent lobe when looking toward the sun. Without it, the underside "appears almost black" ([UE shading models](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/shading-models-in-unreal-engine); [UE forum](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/foliage-shading-in-4-7/17527/10)). Two more pieces of practice matter for crowns. **Transfer normals from a hull, half-sphere or cone proxy to the leaf cards only**, so the crown shades as one volume ([Polycount](https://polycount.com/discussion/comment/2490242)). **Bake AO by depth inside the crown** into vertex colour. Leaf specular should stay dielectric with roughness at or above about 0.4 to avoid sparkle under TAA. Per-instance hue variation driven by a position hash, plus a biome or season noise mask, gives cheap forest-wide variety.

Wind has gone through three generations. The first is Crysis's vertex-colour bending: a main bend `bf=(pos.z*scale+1)^2-1`, renormalised to preserve length, plus a detail bend driven by triangle waves with per-leaf phase in vertex colour ([GPU Gems 3 ch.16](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems3/part-iii-rendering/chapter-16-vegetation-procedural-animation-and-shading-crysis)). The second is hierarchical pivots, as in SpeedTree's sway, branch, ripple and tumble levels, and Pivot Painter 2, which stores per-element pivot and parent index in textures and rebuilds up to 4 levels ([SpeedTree wind](https://docs.unity3d.com/speedtree-modeler/manual/wind-legacy.html); [Pivot Painter 2](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/pivot-painter-tool-2.0-in-unreal-engine)). The third is bone skinning. Because this engine merges plants into per-chunk meshes, the right fit is to bake per-vertex pivot, branch level and phase into the merged vertices at meshing time. Apply trunk sway from one noise sample per plant origin, a rotation about the branch pivot, and leaf flutter along the normal. Above all, **evaluate the same function at the previous frame's time in the motion-vector prepass**. Without that, Bevy's TAA smears moving leaves.

Alpha handling is already ahead of most hobby engines, because coverage-preserving mips (Castaño's bisection method, [The Witness blog](https://www.ludicon.com/castano/blog/articles/computing-alpha-mipmaps/)) stop distant crowns thinning out. Two cheap additions are left. The first is derivative-sharpened alpha, `(a - cutoff)/max(fwidth(a),1e-4) + 0.5`, which turns the leaf edge into a roughly one-pixel ramp. It is attributed to Ben Golus's alpha-to-coverage article, which could not be fetched to confirm the exact form ([Golus](https://bgolus.medium.com/anti-aliased-alpha-test-the-esoteric-alpha-to-coverage-8b177335ae4f)). Alpha-to-coverage itself needs MSAA, and Bevy's TAA path does not use MSAA, so sharpened alpha test plus TAA is the right combination here. The second is a **screen-door dithered crossfade** between LOD tiers, a per-instance `fade` compared against a 4x4 Bayer pattern with the complementary pattern on the incoming LOD, which TAA resolves into a smooth blend.

## Alpha-tested triangles are the hidden tax inside 30 million

Foliage is costly for three reasons that compound. Discard disables early-Z. The empty part of each card is pure overdraw. And GPUs shade in 2x2 pixel quads, so sub-pixel triangles waste most of their fragment work (a general GPU fact, not sourced to a fetched document here). Epic states that masked materials cost far more than opaque ones and that geometric foliage "has shown to be faster with Nanite than card approaches" ([Epic Nanite content docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/working-with-naniteenabled-content)). A merged tier-2 chunk seen from 2 km is almost certainly dominated by sub-pixel triangles, which is the strongest argument for impostors.

The standard mitigation is a **depth prepass running the alpha-tested shader once, depth-only, followed by a main pass that only shades surviving pixels**. Bevy 0.16 notes that its depth prepass lets the main pass skip hidden fragments, though vertex and depth-test cost remain ([Bevy 0.16](https://bevy.org/news/bevy-0-16/)). The engine already runs SSAO, which in Bevy requires depth and normal prepasses, so the prepass exists. What needs checking is how much work the leaf prepass fragment shader does. It should be a single alpha fetch with no lighting, and the main-pass leaf shader should avoid re-running expensive work on pixels that will fail.

GPU-driven rendering cuts CPU cost by a lot and GPU cost by a little. The SIGGRAPH 2015 Assassin's Creed Unity / RedLynx pipeline culls instances and then 64-triangle clusters against a Hi-Z pyramid. CPU draw calls dropped by **1-2 orders of magnitude** and the game handled roughly 10x the instances at 75% of the CPU cost. The overall geometry saving was **under 10%**, although **30-80% of shadow triangles** were culled ([pdf4pro transcription](https://pdf4pro.com/view/gpu-driven-rendering-pipelines-7a5c90.html) of [SIGGRAPH 2015 slides](https://advances.realtimerendering.com/s2015/aaltonenhaar_siggraph2015_combined_final_footer_220dpi.pdf)). Bevy 0.16 ships GPU transform and culling, multi-draw indirect and a mesh allocator. The heavy section of the Caldera Hotel scene went from **33.55 ms to 10.16 ms**. However, **MDI and GPU culling run only on Vulkan**, DX12 and Metal get GPU transforms only, custom-WGSL materials stay CPU-driven unless marked `#[bindless]`, and the two-phase occlusion culling is experimental and can wrongly cull visible meshes ([Bevy 0.16](https://bevy.org/news/bevy-0-16/)). The engine's per-chunk merged meshes already keep draw counts low, so the CPU win from instancing is small. The real limitation is granularity. A chunk is culled, LOD-selected and shadow-cast as one unit, and every tree in it carries duplicated vertices. Species templates plus per-tree instances (Bevy auto-instancing with `MeshTag` for per-instance data, or a custom compute-cull node writing indirect args) would give per-tree LOD and much less vertex memory. Merged meshes are worth keeping until the impostor tier has removed the far load, because the profiler will then show whether near-field granularity matters.

Shadows are where triangle counts multiply. Every cascade redraws its casters, so tier-0 and tier-1 geometry inside the 250 m range is drawn once per covered cascade, alpha-test included. Ways to cut this, in order of effort: use a cheaper shadow fragment shader with a higher alpha mip bias in outer cascades. Render the outer cascade or cascades from the **solid inner crown volumes plus trunks only**. That is an inference, but the volumes are exactly the opaque proxy Nanite voxels approximate. Disable wind in distant cascades, as Unreal does for its virtual shadow maps ([UE Nanite Foliage](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/nanite-foliage)). And update far cascades every N frames when the sun moves slowly. Impostors that write per-pixel depth can cast and receive shadows and take part in SSAO ([Arycama](https://arycama.github.io/2024/10/05/Imposters.html)). Serious Sam 4 uses its impostor depth arrays for exactly this purpose ([SS4 slides](https://gpuopen.com/gdc-presentations/2019/gdc-2019-agtd2-4-million-acres-serious-sam-4.pdf)).

## Shipped open worlds use a three-layer far field the planet lacks

The placement half of the problem is solved the same way everywhere. Horizon Zero Dawn places all nature at runtime on the GPU, **500+ asset types and 100k+ objects at about 250 µs average GPU load**. A painted density map is discretised by a precomputed dither pattern whose points carry ranked thresholds, generated to "even spread thresholds" and "maximize 2D distance", and scaled to each asset's footprint. A point spawns when density exceeds its threshold. Species with the same footprint share one pattern through a two-sided threshold test, so they never overlap and need no read-back. The system is "deterministic" and "locally stable" ([HZD GDC 2017 slides](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/GDC2017_VanMuijden_GPUBasedProceduralPlacementInHorizonZeroDawn.pdf)). The engine's "thinned by rank" approach is structurally this, and it carries the key property: **lowering density only removes points**, so every distant set is a strict subset of the near set and nothing re-randomises as you approach. HZD's two-sided threshold partitioning is worth adding for species that share a footprint class. Ghost Recon Wildlands suggests a cascade for different footprint classes: place big trees first, then reject small-plant candidates near them, which is easy on CPU because each chunk's tree list is already known ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/vegetation-generation-in-ghost-recon-wildlands/)). Wildlands also **switches to impostors sooner where vegetation is denser**, which this engine can copy cheaply by scaling tier distances with local density.

The rendering half is where the engine falls short. Serious Sam 4, the closest documented analogue at 128 x 128 km with 50-100 km vistas, renders vegetation in layers: bare terrain, **forest imposters**, near trees, misc plants, grass at about 7 triangles per blade, and flowers. Trees sit on a **jittered ~8 m grid, roughly 16,000 per 1 km block**. Each block's impostors are a pregenerated grid of 128 x 128 quads at **2 triangles per tree**, reshaped from a per-tree texture. They use 8-direction views with perspective (parallax) compensation, blending the 2 nearest, and texture arrays for albedo, subsurface, normal, AO and depth. Beyond impostor range the "far forest" is **splatted into terrain** ([SS4 slides](https://gpuopen.com/gdc-presentations/2019/gdc-2019-agtd2-4-million-acres-serious-sam-4.pdf)). Octahedral or hemi-octahedral impostors are the more general format. Atlas frames are captured over a hemi-octahedron and the three nearest are blended. They are drawn on a 5-vertex convex hull rather than a quad, and frames are chosen per object, not per vertex. Texture arrays are preferred over atlases to avoid mip bleeding ([Arycama](https://arycama.github.io/2024/10/05/Imposters.html)). A three.js demo draws 200k trees with mesh LODs out to 100 units and impostors beyond, with no crossfade. It switches when the projected tree is no larger than the bake resolution, which hides the pop ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/a-forest-of-octahedral-impostors/85735)). No source in the notes measures impostor versus mesh frame time, so the expected gain below is an estimate.

Bruneton and Neyret formalised the far layer as a view- and light-dependent "shader-map" on the terrain that accounts for sub-pixel crowns and blends seamlessly into near trees ([CGF 2012](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/v31i2pp373-382)). A practical approximation fits the engine's baked biome cube map. Add channels for forest coverage, canopy height and average canopy colour. In the terrain shader, perturb the normal and AO by canopy height, and **darken at grazing view angles** to mimic shadowed gaps between crowns. On far LODs, optionally **displace terrain vertices by coverage x canopy height** so ridgelines get a forest silhouette. The transition should conserve energy: fade the splat in as impostor density fades out, and match the impostor's averaged albedo to the splat colour.

Ground cover belongs on the GPU. Ghost of Tsushima generates Bézier-curve blades per tile in compute. Blades use about 15 vertices near and 7 far, Voronoi clumping, outward-tilted normals that blend toward the terrain normal at distance, view-space widening of edge-on blades, and terrain colour that blends toward grass-top colour at distance to hide thinning ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1027033/Advanced-Graphics-Summit-Procedural-Grass); [Unity-Grass reimplementation](https://cainrademan.github.io/Unity-Grass/)). Its reported cost of about 2.5 ms is from secondary blogs and unverified. Serious Sam 4 uses pre-batched grids whose vertex shader samples the terrain splat so grass takes its albedo from the floor ([SS4 slides](https://gpuopen.com/gdc-presentations/2019/gdc-2019-agtd2-4-million-acres-serious-sam-4.pdf)). Forest floors stop looking flat through layered placement keyed to canopy density: undergrowth near trunks, grass suppressed under dense canopy, and a duff material plus AO darkening under crowns. The canopy density map also gives a cheap sky-occlusion term for ambient light, which SSAO's small radius cannot provide.

## Planet terrain: morph the seams, cull the horizon, keep precision local

The engine's terrain design follows the documented mainstream. KSP 2's PQS+ generates "output mesh positions relative to the floating origin, instead of relative to planet center" ([KSP Developer Insights #12](https://www.kerbalspaceprogram.com:443/news/dev-diaries-developer-insights-12-planet-tech)). Storing per-chunk f64 origins with f32 offsets and subtracting the camera in f64 is the same idea at chunk granularity. Plain f32 relative to the planet centre resolves only about 0.5 m at Earth radius, so this is mandatory. The same logic applies to noise evaluation and texture UVs at level 18: noise should be evaluated in f64 or on chunk-local lattice offsets, and UVs derived from f64 world position modulo the tiling period, not from planet-scale f32. Outerra showed that a **reversed 32-bit float depth buffer covers the roughly 9 decades a planet scene needs**, slightly better than 24-bit logarithmic depth. Log depth written in the fragment shader cost 8-10% ([Outerra](https://outerra.blogspot.com/2012/11/maximizing-depth-buffer-range-and.html)), and Cesium kept a few frustums only for extreme orbital cases ([Cesium](https://cesium.com/blog/2018/05/24/logarithmic-depth/)). Bevy's default reverse-Z with an infinite far plane should therefore suffice; confirm the depth format in the 0.16 source. Physics needs its own origin near the player. Star Citizen found that 64-bit positioning forced a physics refactor ([GamersNexus](https://gamersnexus.net/gg/2622-star-citizen-sean-tracy-64bit-engine-tech-edge-blending)).

The missing pieces are smoothness and selectivity. CDLOD selects quadtree nodes by true 3D distance and **morphs vertices toward the parent grid over the outer part of each range**, giving pop-free transitions without stitching ([CDLOD](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD)). Geometry clipmaps do the same with a blend band of width n/10 ([GPU Gems 2 ch.2](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry)). With CPU meshing, the CDLOD idea becomes one extra attribute: store each vertex's parent-interpolated height (and normal) and lerp in the vertex shader by camera distance. Skirts stay as a safety net. Chunked LOD and Cesium refine by **screen-space error** (geometric error projected by distance) ([Cesium forum](https://community.cesium.com/t/understanding-geometric-error/8480)). Measuring per-chunk error during meshing lets oceans and plains stay coarse while mountains refine, which on an Earth-sized planet is mostly ocean and flatland. Distance should be measured to each chunk's height-inclusive bounding volume so peaks do not under-refine. **Horizon culling** against the planet limb is a cheap, high-value cull. It comes from virtual-globe practice (Cozzi and Ring) and was not fetched in this research.

For streaming, the clipmap paper's key trick transfers well. Predict fine heights by upsampling the parent and add only the new octaves as a residual. On the GPU this cut upsampling from 3 ms to 1 ms and normal computation from 11 ms to 0.6 ms in 2005 ([GPU Gems 2 ch.2](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry)). On the CPU it makes per-chunk generation cost roughly constant with depth, and the parent's heights double as the morph targets. Several queue rules are not sourced to any game but are well established: cache 33x33 height grids (about 4.4 KB each) in an LRU, prioritise jobs by screen error x frustum x above-horizon, finish parents before children, keep the parent visible until all four children are ready, cap uploads per frame, and **cancel stale jobs with a generation counter** so orbital-speed flight does not flood the workers. HZD streams about 4 MB/km² of world maps, while SS4 keeps 32 m/sample global maps for elevation, far albedo and vegetation density in 128-200 MB without streaming them ([HZD](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/GDC2017_VanMuijden_GPUBasedProceduralPlacementInHorizonZeroDawn.pdf); [SS4](https://gpuopen.com/gdc-presentations/2019/gdc-2019-agtd2-4-million-acres-serious-sam-4.pdf)). The engine's baked biome cube map is the planet-scale version of SS4's global maps, and it is the natural home for the canopy channels above. Star Citizen v4 derives biomes from temperature and humidity and keeps ground and orbital views consistent to avoid pop ([Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/news/star-citizen-microtech-planet-tech-v4/)). That is the same discipline the cube map enforces. For atmosphere, Hillaire's LUT-based method used in Unreal is the standard ([EGSR 2020](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf14050)). Aerial perspective is what makes distance read correctly on a planet.

Four guardrails apply to the plan. First, profile before item 10. The AC Unity data says GPU-driven culling mostly saves CPU time, and merged chunks already keep CPU cost low. Second, check which wgpu backend Bevy picks on the target Windows machines, because MDI and GPU culling work only on Vulkan ([Bevy 0.16](https://bevy.org/news/bevy-0-16/)). Third, keep tree placement on CPU workers. Colliders for trees within roughly 50-150 m come directly from the same per-chunk list, an advantage HZD's GPU path had to buy back with a copy to CPU ([HZD](https://www.guerrilla-games.com/media/News/Files/GDC2017_VanMuijden_GPUBasedProceduralPlacementInHorizonZeroDawn.pdf)). Fourth, use published scenes as a sanity scale for budgets. Outerra cites about 200k trees in an average forest scene ([Outerra forum](https://forum.outerra.com/index.php?topic=15.0)), SS4 about 16k trees per km². The estimate that follows from those counts is about 1-2k mesh trees near the camera and tens of thousands of impostors, unsourced but consistent with them.

## Conclusion

The research changes the diagnosis more than the toolset. The engine is not slow because cards, merging or CPU meshing are the wrong choices: Horizon, Serious Sam 4 and KSP 2 made the same structural choices. It is spending geometry where games spend pixels. Every shipped large world treats distant trees as a placement problem rendered by textures, impostor quads and terrain shading, and treats triangles as a near-field luxury. The rank-threshold placement the engine already has is the one property that makes this cheap. Because far tree sets are strict subsets of near ones, impostors, canopy splats and meshes can all be driven from one deterministic list without pop-in from re-randomisation.

The forward-looking signal is Unreal 5.7's move toward opaque leaves, volumetric far LOD and per-bone wind. The engine's solid inner crown volumes already point that way. Treating them as the shadow proxy, the occluder and the seed of a future voxel or "fuzzy crown" far LOD is a credible path, which a custom wgpu renderer can follow without Nanite. The main open questions are empirical: the measured triangle split between tiers, and the share of frame time spent in shadow passes versus alpha overdraw. One RenderDoc capture of a dense forest will tell which of the first three items to do first.
