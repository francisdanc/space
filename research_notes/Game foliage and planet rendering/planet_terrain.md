# Planet-scale terrain rendering: LOD, precision, streaming, texturing, atmosphere

Scope: how planet-scale games and engines render and stream whole planets, with notes applied to the target engine: Rust/Bevy 0.16, quadtree cube-sphere, 32x32-vertex chunks with skirts, levels up to 18 (~1 m), CPU meshing on worker threads, f64 world positions with camera-relative f32 rendering, per-vertex splat weights plus a baked biome cube map.

Method note: this was a tool-limited pass of about 18 calls. Primary sources fetched in full were the Outerra depth-buffer post, the Cesium log-depth blog post, the GPU Gems 2 ch.2 geometry clipmaps chapter, and the CDLOD repo abstract. The CDLOD paper PDF and Ulrich's Chunked LOD notes were downloaded, but no PDF text extractor was available, so I could not read their formulas. Items marked **[unverified background]** come from the researcher's prior knowledge and were not confirmed against a source this session. The report writer should treat them as such.

## 1. Per-game / per-engine approaches

### Takeaway
The engines with real technical documentation (Outerra, Cesium, KSP, the GPU Gems clipmaps) all use height-field terrain on a quadtree or nested grids, with distance or screen-space LOD and some form of origin rebasing. No Man's Sky is the outlier: it builds planets from voxels so they can have caves and overhangs. Star Citizen's public material is mostly press coverage rather than technical documentation.

### Cited Findings
- **No Man's Sky.** Innes McKendrick (Hello Games) gave "Continuous World Generation in No Man's Sky" at GDC 2017. It covers "voxel-based world generation, through polygonization and texturing, to eventual population and simulation", plus the "supporting structures allowing this to happen continuously in real-time." — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024265/); free video noted by [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/video-how-continuous-world-generation-works-in-i-no-man-s-sky-i-)
- A related GDC talk on NMS world-building with maths is "Building Worlds Using Math(s)" (Sean Murray). — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1024514/)
- **Star Citizen.** Planet Tech v4 first shipped in stages with Alpha 3.8. It adds terrain texture blending, object scattering and biome transitions, and derives terrain from temperature and humidity instead of parameter sets with overlaid textures. — [Twinfinite (CitizenCon 2949 coverage)](https://twinfinite.net/news/star-citizen-microtech-planet-tech-v4/); [The Impound wiki](https://theimpound.com/blogs/star-citizen-wiki/planet-tech-v4)
- In Star Citizen, assets are tied to biome ranges, with dithering between zones to smooth transitions (secondary fan blog). — [entergamingxp blog](https://entergamingxp.blogspot.com/2020/02/seamless-scaling-from-gas-giants-to.html)
- Star Citizen 64-bit: Sean Tracy (Technical Director) said 64-bit positioning was a major change, that the engine was not fully converted to 64-bit, and that physics had to be refactored along with positioning. — [GamersNexus interview](https://gamersnexus.net/gg/2622-star-citizen-sean-tracy-64bit-engine-tech-edge-blending)
- Star Citizen Object Container Streaming (OCS) is "the umbrella term for all the technology" behind the seamless universe. Objects with no player nearby can have their state frozen, and planets, stations and points of interest are separate containers that stream in. — [Star Citizen Wiki: OCS](https://starcitizen.tools/Object_Container_Streaming)
- Some players complain that v4's procedural biomes lost the distinctive hand-authored biomes of v3. — [RSI Spectrum thread](https://robertsspaceindustries.com/spectrum/community/SC/forum/50259/thread/planet-tech-v3-to-v4-in-2019-it-s-lost-biomes-un-1)
- **Outerra.** The engine refines real elevation data with fractal algorithms down to centimetre scale. "The fractal map computed for quadtree node already contains 3 independent fractal noise channels": one is used for elevation and another for horizontal vertex displacement, which breaks up the regular grid look. — [Outerra blog on gamedev.net: Horizontal displacement](https://gamedev.net/blogs/entry/1957884-horizontal-displacement)
- Outerra: "Majority of algorithms is running on GPU - multichannel fractal computation, generation of procedural terrain textures, vegetation placement etc." — [ModDB Outerra engine progress](https://www.moddb.com/engines/outerra/news/outerra-engine-progress)
- Outerra's preprocessing tool traverses terrain with adaptive Hilbert curves. The developers noted that blurred source data hurt the fractal refinement. — [gamedev.net: A new terrain mapper & compression tool](https://www.gamedev.net/blog/715/entry-2249786-a-new-terrain-mappercompression-tool/)
- **KSP 1.** Terrain is generated by PQS (Procedural Quad System). Krakensbane and FloatingOrigin rebase the origin to the active vessel past a distance threshold. Krakensbane also absorbs vessel velocity above a threshold and applies it to the rest of the universe. Before 0.17, KSP moved the ship through space instead of moving space around the ship, which caused the "Deep Space Kraken". — [KSP Wiki: Krakensbane API](https://wiki.kerbalspaceprogram.com/wiki/API:Krakensbane); [KSP Wiki: Kraken](https://wiki.kerbalspaceprogram.com/wiki/Kraken); [KSP API docs FloatingOrigin](https://kspmoddinglibs.github.io/KSPDocsSite/class_floating_origin.html)
- **KSP 2 (PQS+).** KSP2 uses a modified PQS called PQS+. Quad subdivision is decided much as in KSP1, but "generate the output mesh positions relative to the floating origin, instead of relative to planet center", which keeps vertex values in a reasonable float range. — [KSP Developer Insights #12 – Planet Tech](https://www.kerbalspaceprogram.com:443/news/dev-diaries-developer-insights-12-planet-tech) (from the search-result summary; the page itself returned 404 when fetched)
- A KSP1 bug report describes terrain tearing at certain longitudes, attributed to how PQS works. — [KSP bug tracker #13213](https://bugs.kerbalspaceprogram.com/issues/13213)
- **Cesium (virtual globe).** Cesium World Terrain leaf tiles are pre-generated meshes of the highest-resolution source data. Non-leaf LODs are built with a simplification algorithm that computes geometric error for runtime use. Resolution is about 30–90 m between ±60° latitude and 1,000 m for the whole Earth. — [Cesium World Terrain](https://www.cesium.com/content/cesium-world-terrain)
- Cesium: screen-space error is the geometric error scaled by the tile's estimated screen footprint, and both values are approximations. A lower max SSE forces higher LODs to load from further away. — [Cesium community: Understanding geometric error](https://community.cesium.com/t/understanding-geometric-error/8480); [Cesium community: maxScreenSpaceError](https://community.cesium.com/t/problem-with-the-finding-a-random-suitable-maxscreenspaceerror-value/8698)
- A Cesium forum user says per-tile geometric error is available in 3D Tiles but not in quantized-mesh. This is a user claim, not an official one. — [Cesium community thread](https://community.cesium.com/t/clarifying-geometric-error-in-cesium-world-terrain-quantized-mesh-tiles/45699)
- **Far Cry 4** (large terrain, not a planet; it is the reference for virtual texturing): a 10 km x 10 km world reaches 10 texels/cm with adaptive virtual texturing, which is 20x traditional virtual texturing. — [GDC Vault: Adaptive Virtual Texture Rendering in Far Cry 4 (Ka Chen, 2015)](https://gdcvault.com/play/1021761/Adaptive-Virtual-Texture-Rendering-in)

### Inferences
- The target design (CPU-meshed cube-sphere quadtree, skirts, f64 with camera-relative f32) is closest to KSP2 PQS+ and Ulrich-style chunked LOD. Outerra is the main example of the GPU-generated alternative.
- KSP2's choice to express meshes relative to the floating origin, not the planet centre, matches "store chunk vertices relative to a per-chunk f64 origin, subtract the camera in f64, then upload f32". That is the standard approach.
- NMS-style voxels are worth adopting only if caves and overhangs are a goal. Since on-foot exploration is the end goal, a hybrid (height-field planet plus local voxel or mesh features such as caves and POIs) is the usual compromise. This is an inference, not sourced to a specific game.

Specifics (dual contouring vs marching cubes, region sizes, generation budgets) are unverified. **[unverified background]** NMS is widely described as using voxel regions polygonised with a dual-contouring-like method and generated asynchronously on worker threads.

## 2. LOD metrics, geomorphing, and crack handling

### Takeaway
CDLOD uses quadtree selection by true 3D distance, with vertex morphing over the outer part of each LOD range, and needs neither stitching nor skirts. Geometry clipmaps use nested grids centred on the viewer, a transition band of width n/10, and degenerate triangles against T-junctions. Chunked LOD and Cesium use a precomputed geometric error projected to screen space, plus skirts. On a sphere, the target's mix of distance-based selection and skirts works, but adding CDLOD-style morphing would remove popping.

### Cited Findings
- CDLOD (Strugar, 2009/2010) is "structured around a quadtree of regular grids". Its LOD function uses the precise 3D observer-to-terrain distance, uniform across the mesh. Its transition technique gives smooth results without stitched meshes. It runs on SM3.0+ hardware, and the author claims better triangle distribution than clipmaps and more predictable behaviour. — [fstrugar/CDLOD repo](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD); [paper (JGT)](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/2151237X.2009.10129287); [updated paper PDF](https://github.com/fstrugar/CDLOD/blob/master/cdlod_paper_latest.pdf)
- Geometry clipmaps (Losasso & Hoppe; Asirvatham & Hoppe, GPU Gems 2 ch.2):
  - Nested n×n grids per level (n = 255 default, n = 2^k − 1, odd so that fine perimeters lie on coarse grid points).
  - Rings are built from 12 reusable m×m blocks with m = (n+1)/4, plus fix-up strips and an L-shaped trim.
  - CPU frustum culling of blocks gives a 2–3x reduction at 90° FOV.
  - Only levels whose extent is at least 2.5x the viewer height are active.
  — [GPU Gems 2, ch.2](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry)
- Clipmap transitions: a band of width w = n/10 near each level's outer edge blends fine elevation toward coarse with a linear alpha from 0 to 1. Normals are blended the same way in the pixel shader. Coarse elevation is packed into the same texture to avoid extra vertex fetches. A strip of zero-area triangles along each ring perimeter prevents T-junction cracks. — [GPU Gems 2, ch.2](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry)
- Clipmap performance (2005 hardware):
  - 11 levels of n = 255 at 1024×768 ran at 130 fps, 60 M tris/s.
  - Removing vertex-texture lookups raised that to 185 fps.
  - The 20-billion-sample US dataset compresses to 355 MB and flies at about 90 fps.
  — [GPU Gems 2, ch.2](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry)
- Cesium uses geometric error projected to screen-space error and refines while SSE exceeds a threshold (default discussed on the forum). — [Cesium community](https://community.cesium.com/t/understanding-geometric-error/8480)

### Inferences
- **[unverified background]** Ulrich's Chunked LOD (SIGGRAPH 2002 course notes, [tulrich.com/geekstuff/sig-notes.pdf](https://tulrich.com/geekstuff/sig-notes.pdf)):
  - Each chunk stores its max geometric error δ, and the screen-space error is ρ = δ / D × K, where K = viewport_width / (2·tan(fov_x/2)) and D is the distance to the chunk's bounding volume.
  - A chunk is split when ρ exceeds a pixel tolerance (for example 1–5 px).
  - Skirts hide cracks.
  - Vertex morphing is driven by ρ, so a chunk blends in as it crosses the threshold.
  - The formula is standard, but I could not confirm it against the PDF this session.
- **[unverified background]** CDLOD morph: each LOD level has a range that doubles per level. Within the last portion of the range (the demo uses about the last 30%; the morph start ratio is configurable), `morphK = clamp((dist − morphStart)/(morphEnd − morphStart), 0, 1)`. Each vertex is snapped toward the coarser grid by `morphedXY = xy − frac(xy·gridDim·0.5)·(2/gridDim)·morphK`, so odd vertices slide onto even ones and the patch matches its parent exactly at the boundary. Selection needs the full set of children in range, and a parent can render only some quadrants. Not confirmed from the PDF.
- For a cube-sphere with CPU meshing, CDLOD-style morphing still applies:
  - Store, per vertex, both the fine height and the "parent-interpolated" height (the average of the two even neighbours along the odd axis). Store the matching normals too.
  - Blend in the vertex shader using the camera distance computed in f32 camera-relative space.
  - This removes popping at no extra CPU cost beyond one extra vec3 per vertex.
  - Skirts can stay as a safety net, since morphing makes boundaries match only when neighbours differ by at most one level and both sides are fully morphed at the shared edge.
- Distance-based LOD (CDLOD) versus SSE (chunked LOD/Cesium):
  - On a procedural planet with roughly uniform geometric error per level, the two are almost equivalent, because SSE is just distance scaled by a per-level δ.
  - SSE becomes valuable if per-chunk error is measured (flat plains stay coarse while mountains refine). That saves many chunks on Earth-sized planets with large flat or ocean areas.
- On spheres, the "distance" should be to the chunk's 3D bounding box/sphere (including its min/max height), not to the chunk centre on the base sphere. Otherwise tall mountains under-refine.
- Horizon culling (occlusion by the planet's limb) is a cheap, high-value cull for planets: a chunk whose bounding sphere is entirely behind the horizon plane from the camera can be skipped. **[unverified background]**; covered by Cozzi & Ring, *3D Engine Design for Virtual Globes*, which was not fetched.

## 3. GPU-side generation vs CPU meshing; caching; streaming budgets; prioritisation

### Takeaway
The two production planet engines with GPU-heavy pipelines (Outerra, Proland) generate fractal heights, textures and placement on the GPU. KSP and the target engine mesh on the CPU in jobs. Geometry clipmaps show that per-frame updates can be incremental (toroidal, coarse-to-fine) and that GPU upsampling plus residuals is about 10x faster than the CPU.

### Cited Findings
- Clipmap updates use toroidal (wrap-around) addressing, so a window is never shifted. Each frame only the newly exposed L- or plus-shaped strip is rewritten. Levels update coarse to fine, and coarse levels update rarely. — [GPU Gems 2, ch.2](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry)
- In clipmaps, fine-level elevation is predicted by C1 four-point subdivision upsampling of the coarser level plus a residual, either decompressed or synthesised as Gaussian noise for fractal detail. Moving this work to the GPU cut normal-map computation from 11 ms to 0.6 ms and upsampling from 3 ms to 1.0 ms. CPU decompression at 8 ms remained the bottleneck. — [GPU Gems 2, ch.2](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry)
- Outerra runs multichannel fractal computation, procedural terrain texture generation and vegetation placement mostly on the GPU, computing a fractal map per quadtree node. — [ModDB](https://www.moddb.com/engines/outerra/news/outerra-engine-progress); [gamedev.net Outerra blog](https://gamedev.net/blogs/entry/1957884-horizontal-displacement)
- Star Citizen streams planets, stations and POIs as separate object containers, and freezes the state of objects with no player near. — [Star Citizen Wiki OCS](https://starcitizen.tools/Object_Container_Streaming)
- A 2018 survey notes that recent planetary techniques use quadtrees with a GPU-oriented approach, and cites a GPGPU approach that needs little to no preprocessing. — [arXiv 1803.04612](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.04612.pdf)

### Inferences (applied to the target engine)
- **Upsample-plus-residual is the key trick to borrow from clipmaps.** When a child chunk at level L+1 is generated:
  - Seed its "coarse" heights by bilinear or 4-point interpolation of the parent's cached height grid. These are exactly the morph targets from section 2.
  - Evaluate only the extra noise octaves that matter at level L+1, and add them.
  - This reuses the parent's work. It suits fBm, since octaves above a level's Nyquist frequency are invisible anyway.
  - Cost per chunk becomes roughly constant instead of growing with the octave count.
- **Cache heights, not just meshes.** Keep an LRU of chunk height grids (33×33 f32, about 4.4 KB) keyed by (face, level, x, y). Rebuilding a mesh from cached heights after eviction is cheap, and parent grids feed the children's morph targets.
- **Prioritise requests** by `(screen-space error or 1/distance) × (in-frustum ? 1 : small) × (above-horizon ? 1 : 0)`, and always finish parents before children. Cap in-flight jobs (for example N_workers × 2). Cap per-frame mesh uploads with a count or byte budget so GPU upload doesn't hitch the frame. Keep the parent visible until all four children are ready (the standard chunked-LOD rule).
- **Cancel stale jobs** with a generation counter or cancellation flag. Fast flight at orbital speeds otherwise floods the queue with chunks that are no longer needed.
- **GPU compute generation** (a Bevy compute shader writing heights to a storage buffer) is an option once noise is expensive. Collision and physics still need CPU-side heights near the player, so a hybrid is common: the GPU for visuals at far levels and the CPU for the near levels, used for physics too. This is inference, not sourced.

- I found no source with concrete per-frame time-slice budgets from shipped planet games (for example "X chunks/frame" or "Y ms/frame").
- KSP2's job-system and Burst details could not be read; the page returned 404 on direct fetch.

## 4. Precision: floating origin, camera-relative rendering, depth range

### Takeaway
Use f64 for simulation and world positions, and express rendered vertices relative to a nearby origin (the KSP approach) or to the camera. For depth, Outerra recommends a reversed 32-bit float depth buffer: it stays consistent over 9 decades (Bevy already uses reverse-Z with infinite far by default). Logarithmic depth is the portable fallback, but it costs about 8–10% when written in the fragment shader.

### Cited Findings
- Outerra:
  - A standard 1/z depth mapping gives about 4 decades of usable precision. A planetary scene, from grass blades to mountains hundreds of km away, needs about 9.
  - A reversed 32-bit float depth buffer is slightly better than a 24-bit log buffer and stays consistent across all 9 decades.
  - In OpenGL, the default [-1, 1] remap adds a 0.5 bias that destroys the gain, so it needs `glDepthRangedNV(-1,1)` (glClipControl did the same later).
  — [Outerra: Maximizing Depth Buffer Range and Precision (2012)](https://outerra.blogspot.com/2012/11/maximizing-depth-buffer-range-and.html)
- Outerra log depth formulas:
  - `z = 2*log(w*C+1)/log(far*C+1) - 1; z *= w`.
  - Linearised version: `logz = log(w*C+1)*FC` with `FC = 1/log(far*C+1)`, then `gl_Position.z = (2*logz-1)*w` and `gl_FragDepth = logz`.
  - C = 0.01 gives about 10 m of near-linear region, which suits FPS views.
  - Decode: `(exp(d/FC)-1)/C`.
  - A near/far variant from Thatcher Ulrich: `2*log(w/near)/log(far/near) - 1`.
  — [Outerra 2012](https://outerra.blogspot.com/2012/11/maximizing-depth-buffer-range-and.html)
- Log depth cost and recommendation:
  - Writing depth in the fragment shader cost about 10% on an Nvidia 460GTX (30 → 27 fps) and about 8% on an AMD 7850 (48 → 44).
  - Vertex-shader-only log depth cost nothing, but interpolates incorrectly on large triangles.
  - Recommendation: reversed FP32 where available. Otherwise use log depth and write fragment depth only for coarsely tessellated objects.
  — [Outerra 2012](https://outerra.blogspot.com/2012/11/maximizing-depth-buffer-range-and.html)
- Kemen's original 2009 log-depth formula is `f(z) = log(C*z+1)/log(C*Far+1)*K`. A fix from Ysaneya for thin or large triangles near the camera disables fast-Z, with negligible cost. — [gamedev.net Logarithmic Depth Buffer](https://gamedev.net/blogs/entry/2001520-logarithmic-depth-buffer); [Floating point depth buffers](https://gamedev.net/blogs/entry/2074023-floating-point-depth-buffers)
- Cesium moved from multi-frustum (3 frustums) to a hybrid with log depth (v1.45):
  - An Everest scene dropped from 137 draw calls (26 duplicates) to 111.
  - It removed 10–40 duplicate draws and per-frustum clears and passes, and allowed a near plane of 0.1–1 m.
  - Log depth needs fragment-depth writes for large triangles, which disables early-Z.
  - Extreme cases still z-fight: planes 300 m apart seen from about 64,000 km. These are handled by keeping a few frustums (`logarithmicDepthFarToNearRatio`).
  — [Cesium blog: Logarithmic Depth (2018)](https://cesium.com/blog/2018/05/24/logarithmic-depth/)
- KSP uses origin rebasing past a distance threshold plus velocity rebasing (Krakensbane). KSP2 generates PQS mesh positions relative to the floating origin. — [KSP Wiki Krakensbane](https://wiki.kerbalspaceprogram.com/wiki/API:Krakensbane); [KSP Dev Insights #12](https://www.kerbalspaceprogram.com:443/news/dev-diaries-developer-insights-12-planet-tech)
- Star Citizen had to refactor physics together with 64-bit positioning. — [GamersNexus](https://gamersnexus.net/gg/2622-star-citizen-sean-tracy-64bit-engine-tech-edge-blending)

### Inferences (applied to the target engine)
- **[unverified background]** Bevy's 3D camera defaults to reverse-Z with an infinite far plane and a Depth32Float buffer. Per Outerra's analysis, that already covers the 9 decades needed for planet scale, so log depth is unnecessary. Verify in the Bevy 0.16 source (`PerspectiveProjection`, `CORE_3D_DEPTH_FORMAT`).
- Mesh precision recipe:
  - Each chunk stores an f64 origin (for example its centre on the sphere) and f32 vertex offsets from that origin. Offsets are at most chunk size; level 0 on Earth is about 10,000 km, still fine because those chunks are only seen from far away.
  - Each frame, set the chunk entity's Transform to `(chunk_origin_f64 − camera_f64) as f32`, with the camera at the origin.
  - Level-18 chunks are about 32 m across, so f32 offsets are far below a millimetre.
  - Equivalent to KSP2's approach, but per chunk rather than per floating origin.
- f32 has about 7 significant digits, so planet-centre-relative f32 positions on Earth (6.37e6 m) only resolve about 0.5 m. That is why per-chunk or camera-relative origins are required for 1 m vertices. This is standard numerics, not sourced to a game.
- Noise evaluation for level-18 chunks must also run in f64, or in f32 on chunk-local coordinates with per-chunk integer lattice offsets, to avoid quantised terrain. On the GPU, this is where double-precision emulation (df64: two-float arithmetic) or integer lattice offsets come in. **[unverified background]**
- Physics (Avian/Rapier) in f32 needs its own local origin near the player. That is the Star Citizen lesson: positioning and physics have to move together.

## 5. Texturing at scale

### Takeaway
Splatting (per-vertex or per-pixel material weights) is the baseline. Virtual texturing (Far Cry 4 adaptive VT: 10 texels/cm over 10 km², 20x classic VT) is the high-end option, but it is complex. Planet engines generate procedural textures on the GPU (Outerra) and derive biomes from climate fields (Star Citizen v4: temperature and humidity).

### Cited Findings
- Far Cry 4 adaptive virtual texturing, 10 km × 10 km world:
  - It reaches 10 texels/cm, 20x traditional VT. MegaTexture and procedural VT lose texel resolution in large open worlds.
  - The terrain VT is a 512,000-pixel-square virtual texture generated procedurally at runtime. Distant ground is rendered over multiple frames, with 8x anisotropic filtering.
  — [GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1021761/Adaptive-Virtual-Texture-Rendering-in); [Game Watch (JP)](https://game.watch.impress.co.jp/docs/news/20150308_691757.html)
- Star Citizen v4 derives terrain and texture distribution from temperature and humidity, with texture blending and biome transitions. — [Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/news/star-citizen-microtech-planet-tech-v4/)
- Outerra generates procedural terrain textures on the GPU. — [ModDB](https://www.moddb.com/engines/outerra/news/outerra-engine-progress)
- Clipmaps store normals in a separate map because 32-bit float textures lack efficient bilinear filtering. — [GPU Gems 2, ch.2](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems2/part-i-geometric-complexity/chapter-2-terrain-rendering-using-gpu-based-geometry)

### Inferences (applied to the target engine)
- The current design (per-vertex splat weights plus a baked biome cube map) matches what a solo-developer planet engine can sustain.
- Splat weights per vertex give 1 m resolution at level 18, but very blurry material boundaries at coarse levels. Look up the biome cube map per pixel at far distances, and per-vertex weights up close.
- Use height-based blending (material height maps) so 1 m-scale per-vertex weights still give sharp, natural transitions up close.
- Triplanar mapping costs 3x samples per layer. Use it only on steep slopes (cliffs) or near the camera; use simple planar UVs on a local tangent frame elsewhere.
- **Distance-dependent texture scale.** Blend two tiling scales (for example 1 m and 16 m), or use a stochastic or hex tiling at the far scale, to kill visible tiling from altitude.
- **Orbit colour map.** At high altitude, swap material splatting for the baked cube map's albedo (an averaged material colour per biome). The cube map then doubles as the "colour map from orbit".
- **UV precision.** Derive UVs from f64 world position modulo the tile period on the CPU per chunk, or from per-chunk offsets. Do not use planet-scale f32 UVs, which have the same 0.5 m quantisation problem as positions.
- Runtime virtual texturing (as in Far Cry 4 or UE RVT) would only be worth considering later. Bevy has no built-in VT.

- I did not fetch details of the Far Cry 4 indirection and page-cache sizes, nor any orbit-colour-map approach from a shipped planet game (KSP2's "graphics of early access" insight was not read).

## 6. Atmosphere, clouds and aerial perspective at planet scale

### Takeaway
Hillaire 2020 (used in Unreal Engine) is the current practical standard. It needs a few small LUTs (transmittance, multiple scattering, sky-view, and a 3D aerial-perspective volume) that are cheap to rebuild every frame, so atmosphere parameters can change dynamically. Reference implementations exist. Ground-from-space views need care, because some implementations don't handle a camera outside the atmosphere.

### Cited Findings
- Hillaire, "A Scalable and Production Ready Sky and Atmosphere Rendering Technique" (Computer Graphics Forum / EGSR 2020):
  - It uses a small set of LUTs and parameterisations for sky and aerial perspective, plus a real-time multiple-scattering approximation.
  - Atmosphere composition can change dynamically without heavy LUT updates. The method is used in Unreal Engine.
  — [EG Digital Library](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf14050); [paper PDF](https://diglib7.eg.org/bitstream/handle/10.1111/cgf14050/v39i4pp013-022.pdf)
- Sky multiple scattering comes from a new LUT, while clouds use a different method. — [Hillaire SIGGRAPH 2020 slides](https://blog.selfshadow.com/publications/s2020-shading-course/hillaire/s2020_pbs_hillaire_slides.pdf)
- One implementation uses a 64×64×32 aerial-perspective LUT without volume shadow, or 200×150×32 with volume shadow. The AirGuanZ DX11 implementation notes that the sky may look wrong with the camera outside the atmosphere, because it doesn't cover planet scale. — [AirGuanZ/AtmosphereRenderer](https://www.Github.com/AirGuanZ/AtmosphereRenderer); [hoffstadt/pl-sky](https://github.com/hoffstadt/pl-sky)
- Frostbite's earlier physically based sky and clouds work (SIGGRAPH 2016). — [Frostbite PBS sky/clouds 2016](https://media.contentapi.ea.com/content/dam/eacom/frostbite/files/s2016_pbs_frostbite_sky_clouds.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[unverified background]** Bevy 0.16 ships a built-in `Atmosphere` component, added in 0.16, based on Hillaire 2020. Its first version was designed for ground-level cameras, and from-space rendering may need custom work. Verify against the Bevy 0.16 release notes.
- At planet scale, aerial perspective is what sells distance. Apply the 3D AP LUT to terrain in the forward or deferred pass. From orbit, ray-march the atmosphere shell against the planet sphere analytically (Bruneton-style precomputed scattering also supports space views). **[unverified background]**
